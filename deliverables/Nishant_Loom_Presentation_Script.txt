LIKELY EVALUATOR QUESTIONS

Q: Why is your overall margin different from the average of the Operating Margin column?
A: Because a simple average weights every row equally, so a city selling 4,000 units counts as much as one selling 12,000. The portfolio margin is total operating profit $3,173,632 divided by total sales $8,684,028, which is 36.5%. The simple row average is 36.3%, which sits 0.2 percentage points away from the weighted figure. Every margin in the workbook is a ratio of sums.

Q: Is the West really less profitable, or is that just FizzySip?
A: Both readings are in the data and I cannot separate them. The West is 32.6% against 36.5% for the portfolio, but FizzySip operates only in the West, and BevCo, the highest-margin retailer, operates only in the South. Retailer and region are almost the same variable here. What survives that caveat is the within-brand comparison: Coca-Cola earns 35.2% in the West versus 43.8% in the Southeast, so the gap is not just brand mix. It needs a cell-level review.

Q: Can you claim seasonality from this data?
A: No. I have twelve months from a single year, so I describe within-year patterns: December is the highest sales month, March the lowest, July the highest units month. To claim recurring seasonality I would need at least two or three years, and the workbook contains no prior-year actuals, which is why the trend chart has no prior-year line.

Q: Does the price-volume correlation prove that lowering price raises volume?
A: No. The pooled correlation is 0.25 and it is positive, which is the opposite of a simple demand curve, because expensive markets are also high-volume markets. It is an association across cities and months, confounded by retailer, location and time. Elasticity needs a controlled test with a comparison group, which is exactly what I recommend.

Q: What would you do next with better data?
A: Three additions would change the recommendations from investigation to action: cost of goods and operating cost per unit, so margin differences can be attributed; store counts or distribution coverage per city, so sales can be normalised into productivity; and two more years of history, so seasonality and price changes can be separated from growth. Promotion and availability data would complete the picture.
